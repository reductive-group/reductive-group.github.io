A phonetic alphabet represents the sounds of its language with its letters. To truly earn the name phonetic it should probably do a more faithful job than the Latin alphabet does for English. This can be measured in two directions: how possible it is to pronounce unknown words from their spelling, and how possible it is to spell unknown words from their pronunciation. In this sense, it is important for rook script to be a phonetic alphabet, or to become one if it is not already.

Many people have already put a lot of work into representing English words phonetically. Pronunciation guides in dictionaries, IPA transcriptions, and Wikipedia’s [phonetic respelling conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key&oldid=1348177802) are all examples.

If this is the goal, why not simply take a dictionary’s pronunciation guide as the spelling? There are a few drawbacks to this approach. Noticeably, most dictionaries will include multiple pronunciations for many words. If you have to pick a most correct or most favored pronunciation (and I believe you should), by what basis should you do so?

Answer: read the word out loud and listen to yourself

However I also find dictionary pronunciation guides to be unsatisfying. Two problems: one they fail to capture the essence of a word in its spoken form. This is a subjective judgment that could maybe be said of the official spellings in rook script, but I believe most pronunciation guides err on the narrow side of the bridge by notating distinctions that are difficult to hear and reproduce (see the different vowel systems in MW, American Heritage, etc); or on the broad side by eliding distinctions that are conceptually firm (see google). Right in the middle, to me, is the IPA in use at Wiktionary and in OED American. However I find IPA to be lacking because it is usually poorly typeset, and was in birth an international alphabet designed to aid in cross-linguistic comparison. This makes it an aesthetically weak fit for what I want to accomplish.

A good example word to see where many of these approaches fail is “autism”.
