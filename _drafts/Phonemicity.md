A phonetic alphabet represents the sounds of its language with its letters. To truly earn the name phonetic it should probably do a more faithful job than the Latin alphabet does for English. This can be measured in two directions: how possible it is to pronounce unknown words from their spelling, and how possible it is to spell unknown words from their pronunciation. In this sense, it is important for rook script to be a phonetic alphabet, or to become one if it is not already.

Many people have already put a lot of work into representing English words phonetically. Pronunciation guides in dictionaries, IPA transcriptions used by linguists and auto-captioning systems, and Wikipedia’s [phonetic respelling conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key&oldid=1348177802) are all examples. So why not use their work?

The truth is that choosing a convention for spelling words is a translation from sounds to symbols. Every translation involves a mountain of decisions to make, and should be guided by a philosophy. I found that the philosophy of transcription I want for rook script is different than anything that currently exists. This page is an attempt to summarize that philosophy

## Fitting my dialect

- It’s been said that Hangul fits Korean like a glove, a better fit than any alphabet to any language
- This was the dream I had for rook script in English. Despite lacking syllabic morphophonemes to the extent Korean does, perhaps a syllabic—certainly a more phonetic—alphabet could fit English better than the current one does. It could use fewer letters per word and be faster to read, giving a stronger subjective feeling that reading a word is truly knowing it. (To the extent that conventional Latin spelling does this for English it’s through etymology: reading an unfamiliar word gives very strong clues to its derivation. And, if you’re sufficiently familiar with its source language, similarly strong clues to its meaning.)
- I genuinely think I’ve succeeded. I think you you have to learn to use rook script to understand what I mean.

## Intrinsic use of syllable boundaries

- Being a syllabic alphabet, one major decision for rook script was how to take advantage of syllable boundaries
- In the flow of speech, syllable and word boundaries are not naturally marked or translinguistically definable
- The linguistics standard is onset maximization. I believe this soundly fails for English, especially American English
- The standard used in Korean is referred to as “morphophonemic”. Unfortunately I do not understand well what that means for Korean speakers, but I don’t see a way to consistently prioritize morphology in syllable boundaries for English. There are so many meaningful components of words that take non-systematic forms, I don’t believe most of them can be accommodated. (In addition to boundaries in most compound words, there are a handful of prefixes and suffixes that are usually respected in official rook spellings, largely because they don’t excessively interfere with the following.)
- Instead, syllable boundaries are primarily used to express phonetic and phonological information. First and foremost they reflect stress, and they can also imply letters’ sounds are especially influenced by those of particular surrounding letters.

## Morphology, phonemics, and phonetics

- kind of the meat of what I want to talk about here
- Rook script intends to be “phonemic” rather than phonetic
- and to pay relatively little attention to morphology, reserving it as a tiebreaker in most cases
