English is a stressed language. When speaking English, some syllables are held for longer, spoken louder, and voiced at a higher pitch than other syllables. That much we can say for sure. 

It can be difficult to explain which syllables will be stressed in a sentence. One challenge of reading aloud is deciding how much to stress each syllable and each word relative to others. As a way of disentangling stress, and to explain how rook script treats it, I delineate between word stress and sentence stress. Every syllable of every word has instrinsic stress properties—it can have primary stress, it can be unstressed, etc. This is word stress, and it’s the focus of this page (and of rook script). Word stress helps determine sentence stress, which also has to account for other factors like placing emphasis in a sentence or maintaining a consistent rhythm. Because sentence stress involves factors that are not stable across contexts, I believe accounting for it in spelling would be unnecessarily difficult. I also don’t understand it very well and can’t even briefly explain how it works.

Rook script doesn’t even aim to capture all aspects of word-level stress. Instead, it assigns to each syllable one of three levels: primary stress, secondary stress, or no stress. The word “loquacious\|lou·kwei·shis” has all three types: secondary-primary-none. For rook script, primary and secondary stress are defined only relative to adjacent syllables. This is unusual! For example, in the word “lookalike\|luk·ə·laik”, it’s fair to say the first syllable has more stress than the third syllable, so for example [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/lookalike) notates them as having different levels of stress. But according to rook script, the stress pattern is primary-none-primary. This is because, in rook script, secondary stress only refers to syllables that are not unstressed but have less stress than a neighboring syllable. Any syllable that has stress, and more stress than (both) its adjacent syllable(s), is said to have primary stress. 

The official stress pattern of every word is given in the pronunciation columns of the [rook script dictionary](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1Hr1Eyu2BLganoblsPMf3o-_bwgakcLiA). In the dictionary, primary stress is indicated with an acute accent ´, secondard stress with a grave accent `, and unstress syllables have no accent.

Stress interacts with spelling in two ways: limiting the vowels that can appear in unstressed syllables, and setting [syllable boundaries]({% post_url 2026-02-28-Syllables %}).
